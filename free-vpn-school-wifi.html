Are free VPNs safe for school WiFi?

Updated 28 July 2026·5 min read

Short answer: most free VPNs won't get through a school filter at all, and a meaningful share of the ones that do make their money in ways you wouldn't agree to if they asked plainly. But "free is always bad" is lazy advice — there are a couple of genuinely trustworthy free options, and it's worth knowing which is which.

  2. What free actually costs

Why they don't work at school

Running a VPN costs real money — servers, bandwidth, engineering time. A free provider has to cover that somehow, and the cheapest way is to run as few servers as possible with as many users crammed onto them as will fit.

That creates exactly the conditions a school filter loves:

  • A handful of well-known IP addresses. Millions of users behind a small pool of servers means a very short blocklist entry. One line blocks everyone.
  • Enormous popularity. The most-downloaded free VPNs are the first thing any filter vendor adds. Popularity is the problem.
  • Standard protocols only. Obfuscation costs engineering effort, which free products rarely invest in. So the traffic is trivially fingerprinted.
  • Overloaded servers. Even when a connection succeeds, sharing a server with thousands of others means speeds that make video unwatchable.

The mechanics of all of this are covered properly in why your VPN gets blocked at school.

What free actually costs

This is the part that deserves more attention than it gets. Security researchers have repeatedly audited popular free VPN apps and found the same patterns turning up:

  • Logging and selling browsing data. The exact activity you installed a VPN to protect, packaged and sold to data brokers or advertisers. Several major free providers have been caught doing precisely this.
  • Injecting ads and trackers. Some free VPNs modify the pages you load to insert their own advertising or tracking code.
  • Selling your bandwidth. A few free VPNs turn your device into an exit node for other people's traffic. That means strangers' activity leaves the internet from your connection — which can be anything, and it's your IP address attached to it.
  • Weak or fake encryption. Audits have found apps advertising encryption that either implemented it badly or didn't implement it at all.
  • Excessive permissions. Free VPN apps routinely request access to contacts, location and storage that a VPN has no legitimate need for.

A VPN sees all your traffic by design. Handing that to a company whose business model you can't explain is a bigger privacy trade than the one you were trying to fix.

None of this means every free VPN is malicious. It means "free" tells you nothing about safety, and the incentive structure points the wrong way. The useful question is never "is it free" but "how does this company pay its bills, and can I tell?"

The genuinely trustworthy free options

Two are worth knowing about, and we'll be straight that neither is likely to solve your school problem:

Proton VPN Free is the standout. Unlimited data, no ads, no logging, funded by paying subscribers of the paid tier rather than by monetising free users. Genuinely reputable. It's also extremely well known, which means it's on essentially every school blocklist — so it's a great free VPN for a coffee shop and usually useless on school WiFi.

Cloudflare WARP is free, fast, and run by a company whose business plainly doesn't depend on selling your browsing. But it isn't really a VPN in the sense you want — it doesn't hide your location, and many schools block Cloudflare's ranges outright.

So the honest summary: the trustworthy free options are trustworthy precisely because they're big and well-funded, and being big and well-known is exactly what gets a VPN blocked at school. The two things are in tension.

When paying is worth it — and when it isn't

We sell a VPN, so treat this section with appropriate scepticism. Still, the honest cases:

A free VPN is genuinely fine if you're on public WiFi at a café or airport and just want your traffic encrypted, if you're doing something occasional and low-stakes, or if you're happy with Proton's free tier on a network that doesn't filter. Don't pay for something you don't need.

Paying makes sense when you're on a network that actively blocks VPNs, when you need it to work reliably rather than sometimes, when speed matters because you're watching video, or when you want to know who's running the server your entire browsing session passes through.

The last one is the real argument. With a paid service the exchange is legible — money for a service. With a free one you're trusting a company that has decided not to charge you, and you generally can't tell why.

Whatever you land on: read the privacy policy, look up who owns it, and be suspicious of any VPN that can't clearly explain how it makes money.
